# Fisher's fundamental theorem

```
Theory: Fisher's fundamental theorem of natural selection
Original paper (year): Fisher RA. 1930. The Genetical
  Theory of Natural Selection. Oxford: Clarendon Press.
  xiv + 272 pages, 2 colour plates. No DOI. Publisher,
  place, year and pagination checked against the Library
  of Congress record (LCCN 30029177) and against the 1930
  Nature review of the book (the review is not the
  source; it is used only to confirm the imprint and
  page count). Chapter II is titled "The Fundamental
  Theorem of Natural Selection". The geometric model in
  the same book is a separate note
  (fisher-geometric-model). Price GR. 1972. Fisher's
  "fundamental theorem" made clear. Ann Hum Genet
  36:129-140. DOI 10.1111/j.1469-1809.1972.tb00764.x
  is the clarification used in the chapter, not the
  original statement.
One-sentence prediction: The rate of increase in fitness
  of any organism at any time is equal to its genetic
  variance in fitness at that time. This is a partial
  change, equal to the additive genetic variance in
  fitness divided by mean fitness, not a claim that mean
  fitness always rises (Price 1972; Frank and Slatkin
  1992, DOI 10.1016/0169-5347(92)90248-A).
Assumptions: a well-defined sexual generation; fitness
  and additive genetic values scored on the same
  individuals; the theorem describes only that partial
  change, with the rest attributed to the environment,
  dominance and epistasis.
Which fail here: cuttings skip the sexual generation.
  The chapter has no estimate of additive genetic
  variance in fitness in grapevine; not tested in
  grapevine here. A few large-effect domestication loci
  are not a test of the partial-change theorem.
Modern data / statistic: direct estimates of additive
  genetic variance in fitness in wild animals (Bonnet
  et al. 2022, DOI 10.1126/science.abk0853) and in
  plants such as Chamaecrista, as discussed in the
  chapter. Grapevine: not tested here.
What would falsify it: in a system that meets the
  assumptions, a fair measure of the partial change in
  mean fitness due to selection is not equal to the
  additive genetic variance in fitness divided by mean
  fitness.
What we will not claim: that this note is the geometric
  model (that is a separate note). That mean fitness
  always increases. That a grapevine estimate of
  additive variance in fitness appears in the chapter.
```
